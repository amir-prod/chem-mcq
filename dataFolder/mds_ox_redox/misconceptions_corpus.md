## Misconception 1: Students think the freshman-chemistry electron-transfer definition of oxidation/reduction transfers directly and intuitively to organic chemistry without reinterpretation.

**Topic:** Oxidation–reduction in organic chemistry / Transfer from freshman definitions to organic chemistry
**Correct concept:** In organic chemistry, oxidation and reduction are often identified by changes in bonding patterns and electron density, not only by simple ion-electron bookkeeping.
**Expected reasoning:** A student should recognize that organic redox can be described by changes such as loss/gain of hydrogen, addition/loss of oxygen, or changes in electron density around carbon, and then relate those changes back to electron accounting when appropriate.
**Concept category:** general redox concepts
**Population:** Beginning students in introductory organic chemistry
**Source:** Understanding Oxidation–Reduction in Organic Chemistry (1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry/1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry.md)
**Evidence:** "the application of this knowledge is not intuitively transferable in organic chemistry"

## Misconception 2: Students may think reduction of a carbonyl is just 'hydrogenation' or addition of hydrogen, without recognizing the electron-transfer step.

**Topic:** Organic reduction / Interpretation of carbonyl reduction
**Correct concept:** Carbonyl reduction involves gain of electrons by the carbonyl carbon, with protonation occurring afterward to give the reduced product.
**Expected reasoning:** A student should first identify the electron gain at carbon, then see protonation as a separate step that neutralizes charge and completes product formation.
**Concept category:** electron transfer
**Source:** Understanding Oxidation–Reduction in Organic Chemistry (1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry/1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry.md)
**Evidence:** "the 'actual reduction step' ... because each carbon gains one electron (a total of two). The last stage is merely the acquisition of two protons"

## Misconception 3: Students may think the metal reduction of a carbonyl is a single direct hydrogenation rather than an electron donation from the metal followed by protonation/workup.

**Topic:** Clemmensen reduction / Active-metal reduction mechanism
**Correct concept:** Zinc first donates electrons to the carbonyl-derived intermediate; protonation then completes the reduction to the methylene product.
**Expected reasoning:** A student should track electrons from Zn^0 to the substrate, then account for added H+ in the workup as the final protonation step that gives the neutral product.
**Concept category:** active metal reduction
**Source:** Understanding Oxidation–Reduction in Organic Chemistry (1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry/1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry.md)
**Evidence:** "Zinc metal (oxidation state 0) provides the electrons that actually effect the reduction"

**Expected reasoning:** A student should compare oxidation states/electron density at carbon across the steps and see that each step corresponds to further reduction of the carbon center.
**Concept category:** active metal reduction

## Misconception 5: Students may think the Wolff–Kishner reduction is only a reduction of the carbonyl-derived carbon and overlook that the nitrogen atoms are oxidized.

**Topic:** Wolff–Kishner reduction / Role of hydrazone oxidation
**Correct concept:** The Wolff–Kishner reaction is an internal redox/disproportionation in which carbon is reduced and nitrogen is oxidized to N2.
**Expected reasoning:** A student should assign electrons/oxidation states across the hydrazone and see that one part of the molecule loses electron density (nitrogen to N2) while carbon gains it.
**Concept category:** internal redox / disproportionation
**Source:** Understanding Oxidation–Reduction in Organic Chemistry (1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry/1997-understanding-oxidation-reduction-in-organic-chemistry.md)
**Evidence:** "one portion (the nitrogens) is oxidized and the other portion (the carbon) is reduced"
