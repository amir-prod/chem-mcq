### Blueprint

- **Target misconception:** Substances with hydrogen atoms always exhibit hydrogen bonding, so any molecule containing H should have an unusually high boiling point.
- **Correct answer concept:** Hydrogen bonding occurs only when H is directly bonded to N, O, or F; molecules lacking that arrangement rely on weaker dipole-dipole or London dispersion forces and therefore generally have lower boiling points.
- **Expected reasoning:** Students should inspect molecular structures rather than just chemical formulas with H present. They should identify which substance can form hydrogen bonds between molecules and conclude that stronger intermolecular attractions raise the boiling point.
- **Style notes:** Use a simple comparison set such as CH4, HCl, HF, and CO2 or similar small molecules. Ask which has the highest boiling point or which pair differs because of hydrogen bonding. Keep structure recognition central and avoid needing exact boiling point values.

**Explanation:** HF has H directly bonded to F, so its molecules can form hydrogen bonds. CH4, HCl, and CO2 do not have H bonded to N, O, or F, so they cannot hydrogen-bond with themselves in the same way and generally have lower boiling points.

Three liquids have similar molar masses: dimethyl ether (CH3OCH3), acetaldehyde (CH3CHO), and ethanol (CH3CH2OH). Which liquid has the highest boiling point, and why?

- **A.** Dimethyl ether, because the oxygen atom makes its intermolecular forces strongest
- **B.** Acetaldehyde, because its polarity alone gives it the strongest attractions
- **C.** Ethanol, because its molecules can form hydrogen bonds with each other ✓
- **D.** All three should have nearly the same boiling point because their molar masses are similar

**Explanation:** Ethanol has an O-H bond, so ethanol molecules can hydrogen bond to each other.
